How I work through a data problem

  1. Ask the real question. Before touching the data, I figure out what decision the answer will help someone make.
  2. Check the data before trusting it. Duplicates, missing values, an extra game in a season. The small stuff changes the answer.
  3. Build it, then try to break it. I test on data the model hasn't seen and look for the ways it could be fooling me.
  4. Explain it plainly. A result nobody understands doesn't help anyone, so I finish with what it means and what to do next.

AI review insights tool

Python, pandas, regex, a local AI model

Apparel brands get thousands of reviews and no time to read them. I built a pipeline that tags 22,628 clothing reviews by theme, then sends samples from each department to an AI model running on my own laptop, which writes short reports: top complaints, what customers love, and one recommendation.

The hard part: the AI said jackets run small. I added a code-based check that counts “too small” versus “too large” complaints, and the data said they run large. The final design: code handles the numbers, AI handles the text, and a person reviews both.

Why it matters: a brand could fix fabric quality first, and update size guides in departments that run large, instead of guessing from a few angry reviews.

Purchase intent classification

Python, scikit-learn, Random Forest, logistic regression

Using 12,330 real browsing sessions from an online store, I predicted whether a visitor will buy based only on how they browse. Random Forest beat logistic regression on nearly every metric. Page Values was by far the strongest signal, and new visitors converted more often than returning ones (25% vs. 14%).

The hard part: only 15.5% of sessions ended in a purchase, so a model that always guesses “no” is 84% accurate and useless. I balanced the classes in training and judged the models on precision, recall, F1, and ROC-AUC instead.

Why it matters: a store could show offers only to the visitors most likely to buy, instead of to everyone.

77%of real buyers caught, with a 0.93 ROC-AUC

Retail sales & staffing forecast

Python, scikit-learn, time-based validation

As a Starbucks shift leader, I saw how reactive staffing is, so I tested whether busy weeks can be predicted. Using 421,000+ weekly Walmart sales records across 45 stores, I forecast each store's sales and grouped the results into four staffing tiers, each based on that store's own average so smaller stores aren't always marked “low.”

The hard part: a random train/test split would let the model learn from weeks after the ones it's tested on. I split by time instead, training on older weeks and testing on the newest 20%.

Why it matters: a manager could add staff before a busy week and cut hours in slow ones, instead of reacting during the rush.

4.8%average forecast error (MAPE) on 7 months of unseen weeks

OKC Thunder four factors

R, tidyverse, hoopR, logistic regression

Which of basketball's Four Factors (shooting, turnovers, rebounding, free throws) actually drive wins? I analyzed every Thunder regular-season game from 2022–23 through 2025–26, using standardized factors so their effects can be compared directly. Shooting on both ends mattered most, and defense mattered almost as much as offense.

The hard part: checking the data against official standings turned up an 83rd game in 2024–25. I traced it to the NBA Cup final, which doesn't count in the standings, and kept it because it's a real game with real stats.

Why it matters: for a coaching staff, contesting the other team’s shots is worth almost as much as making your own.

Inside my internship

Data Analyst Intern, National League of Cities, Summer 2026. NLC serves city leaders across the country, and its teams rely on Power BI and SharePoint reports to track website traffic and internal work.

The question

Do NLC’s reports give each team what it needs to make decisions, and what would keep them organized and up to date over time?

  1. Listened first. I interviewed stakeholders across teams to understand and document their business intelligence needs for website traffic and internal SharePoint reporting.
  2. Reviewed what existed. I went through the organization’s Power BI environment, looking at how reports were organized and how easy they were to use for decision-making.
  3. Designed a fix that lasts. I built a data stewardship process, with an organizational strategy and change management steps, so reports stay organized and up to date after I leave.
  4. Made the case to leadership. I presented an executive summary of my findings and recommendations to leadership, and delivered a final report on the project.

What I took from it

The tool is rarely the hard part. Good reporting depends on knowing who uses each report, who owns it, and how it stays current. That’s why I start every project by asking what decision the data needs to support.
